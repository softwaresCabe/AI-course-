## 12.1 Evals are the product spec

In traditional software, tests define correctness. In AI systems, **evals** do. A team without evals is guessing; a team with good evals can swap models, rewrite prompts and ship weekly with confidence. **Error analysis** — reading real outputs, categorizing failures, and counting them — is the highest-leverage activity in AI engineering.

Eval types:
- **Code-based (deterministic)**: exact match, regex, JSON-schema validity, unit tests on generated code, SQL result equality. Cheap, reliable — use wherever possible.
- **LLM-as-judge**: a model grades outputs against a rubric (pairwise or pointwise). Scales to open-ended tasks, but must be **validated against human labels**.
- **Human evaluation**: domain experts label a sample; the gold standard and the calibration source for everything else.
- **Online/production signals**: user feedback, acceptance rate, edits, escalations, A/B tests.

## 12.2 Building an eval suite

1. Start from **real usage**: sample production traces or realistic synthetic inputs covering common cases, edge cases and adversarial cases.
2. Do **error analysis**: read ~100 outputs, write notes, cluster failures into categories ("wrong SKU", "ignored dietary modifier", "hallucinated policy").
3. Turn each failure category into **targeted evals** with clear pass/fail criteria.
4. Write **binary or small-scale rubrics** ("Does the answer cite a source for every policy claim? yes/no") — they're more reliable than 1–10 scales.
5. Run evals in **CI** on every prompt/model change; track metrics over time; review regressions.
6. Keep a held-out set to avoid overfitting your prompts to the eval.

**Statistics matter**: with 100 test cases, a 95% confidence interval on an 80% pass rate is roughly ±8 points (`1.96·√(p(1−p)/n)`). Don't celebrate a 2-point improvement on 50 examples. Use paired comparisons on the same items when comparing two systems.

## 12.3 LLM-as-judge done right

- Give the judge the rubric, the input, the output, and (when available) a reference answer.
- Ask for reasoning before the verdict; output structured JSON.
- **Calibrate**: label 50–200 examples by hand and measure judge agreement (accuracy, Cohen's κ, true-positive/true-negative rates). Iterate on the judge prompt until agreement is high.
- Known biases: **position bias** (prefers first/second option — randomize order), **verbosity bias**, **self-preference** (favoring outputs from its own model family). Mitigate with swaps, length controls, and a different judge model.

## 12.4 Benchmarks and their limits

Public benchmarks track progress: MMLU / MMLU-Pro (knowledge), GPQA (graduate-level science), HumanEval and SWE-bench (code), MATH and AIME (math), Humanity's Last Exam (frontier difficulty), LMArena, formerly Chatbot Arena (human pairwise preferences), plus agentic suites. Limitations: **contamination** (test items in training data), **saturation** (top models near ceiling), **Goodharting** (optimizing for the leaderboard), and **poor transfer** to your task. Benchmarks pick candidates; **your evals pick the winner**.

## 12.5 Security: prompt injection and friends

The OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications (2025 edition) is the industry checklist. Highlights:

- **Prompt injection**: *direct* (user tries to override instructions) and *indirect* (instructions hidden in documents, web pages, emails or tool results the model reads). There is no complete fix today; defend in depth:
  - Treat all model inputs from outside as untrusted; delimit them.
  - **Least privilege**: the model can only do what its tools allow; scope credentials per user/tenant.
  - Human confirmation for consequential actions.
  - Don't put secrets in prompts.
  - Classifiers/filters on inputs and outputs; monitoring for anomalies.
  - Architectural patterns that separate untrusted data from privileged planning (e.g. a quarantined model processes untrusted content and returns only constrained, typed data).
- **Improper output handling** (called "insecure output handling" in the 2023 list): never `eval()` model output, render it as raw HTML (XSS), or splice it into SQL/shell.
- **Sensitive information disclosure** and **system prompt leakage**: PII in prompts/logs; secrets or rules in system prompts; cross-tenant leakage via retrieval.
- **Vector and embedding weaknesses**: poisoned or unauthorized-access embeddings in RAG stores.
- **Excessive agency**, **misinformation**, and **unbounded consumption** (denial-of-wallet).
- **Data/model poisoning** and **supply chain** risks (malicious model files — prefer safetensors over pickle).

## 12.6 Alignment and AI safety — the big picture

**Alignment** = getting AI systems to reliably pursue the goals we intend, including in novel situations. Key problems:
- **Specification gaming / reward hacking**: optimizing the letter, not the spirit, of an objective.
- **Goal misgeneralization**: learning a proxy goal that matches training but diverges in deployment.
- **Sycophancy and deception**: telling users what they want to hear; models behaving differently when they believe they're observed.
- **Scalable oversight**: how do humans supervise systems that are better than them at a task? Approaches include AI-assisted evaluation, debate, and decomposition.
- **Dangerous capabilities**: frontier labs run evaluations for cyber, bio/chem, and autonomy risks and tie deployment safeguards to results (e.g. Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy and AI Safety Levels; similar frameworks exist at other labs).

## 12.7 Interpretability

**Mechanistic interpretability** tries to reverse-engineer what networks compute.
- **Superposition**: models represent more features than they have neurons by encoding them in overlapping directions, which makes individual neurons polysemantic.
- **Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) / dictionary learning** decompose activations into many sparse, more interpretable **features**. Anthropic's "Towards Monosemanticity" (2023) showed this on a one-layer model; "Scaling Monosemanticity" (2024) scaled it to Claude 3 Sonnet, found features for concepts like the Golden Gate Bridge, code bugs and deception, and showed steering by clamping them.
- **Circuit tracing / attribution graphs** follow how features interact to produce an output.
- Simpler tools you can use today: **linear probes**, **activation patching**, **logit lens**.
Why it matters: auditing models for hidden goals, debugging failures, and building justified trust.

## 12.8 LLMOps: running AI in production

- **Observability**: trace every request (prompt version, model, inputs, retrieved docs, tool calls, outputs, latency, tokens, cost, user feedback). Tools: Langfuse, LangSmith, Braintrust, Arize Phoenix, Helicone, or OpenTelemetry into your existing stack (e.g. Sentry for errors).
- **Prompt and model versioning**; staged rollouts; A/B tests; quick rollback.
- **Monitoring**: quality metrics from online evals, drift in input distributions, latency SLOs, cost per feature/tenant, rate limits.
- **Data governance**: PII redaction, retention policies, provider data-use terms, regional requirements, and regulations such as the EU AI Act.
- **Model migrations**: when a provider releases a new model, re-run your eval suite before switching — behavior changes.
