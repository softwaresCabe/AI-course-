## 13.1 Frontiers you should be able to discuss

**Multimodality.** Modern frontier models natively take images, documents, audio and sometimes video as input, and many generate images and speech. Vision is typically added by encoding image patches (as in ViT) into tokens the transformer attends to. **Diffusion models** (iteratively denoising random noise into an image, video or audio, often in a learned latent space) and their successors such as flow matching dominate media generation; **real-time voice** models stream audio in and out with low latency — directly relevant to AI voice ordering.

**Computer use and embodied agents.** Agents that operate GUIs from screenshots, browsers, and robots trained with **vision-language-action (VLA)** models extend the agent loop from APIs to the physical and visual world.

**Reasoning and test-time compute.** Reasoning models trade inference compute for accuracy. Open questions: how far this scales, how to make reasoning **faithful** (the stated chain of thought reflecting the actual computation) and efficient, and how to verify answers in domains without automatic checkers.

**Efficiency.** MoE, distillation, quantization, speculative decoding, state-space and hybrid architectures, and specialized hardware keep driving cost per token down — making new applications economical every year.

**Data and synthetic data.** As high-quality human text runs short, synthetic data, self-play, and learning from environments (RL) matter more. Risks include **model collapse** if models train naively on their own outputs.

**Open vs closed models.** Open-weight models lag the frontier somewhat but enable on-prem, fine-tuning and research; the policy debate weighs openness benefits against misuse risks.

**Governance.** The EU AI Act, national AI safety/security institutes, frontier-lab safety frameworks, and export controls on advanced chips shape what can be built and deployed. Engineers increasingly need working knowledge of compliance and documentation (model cards, system cards, risk assessments).

## 13.2 What an AI engineer actually does

Job titles vary — AI Engineer, Applied AI Engineer, ML Engineer (LLM), Forward Deployed Engineer, AI Solutions/Support Engineer — but the work clusters into:

1. **Product integration**: prompts, tools, RAG and agents inside real applications (Weeks 9–11).
2. **Evaluation and quality**: building eval suites, error analysis, judge calibration (Week 12).
3. **Model adaptation**: fine-tuning, distillation, preference tuning (Week 7).
4. **Serving and cost**: latency, throughput, caching, routing, self-hosting (Week 8).
5. **Reliability, security and governance**: guardrails, red teaming, observability, compliance.

Your background is an asset: years of production WMS/outbound/quality-and-recovery engineering map directly onto reliability, observability and failure analysis — exactly what most AI teams lack. Two shipped multi-tenant SaaS products with Next.js/Postgres are a ready-made portfolio canvas.

## 13.3 Building a portfolio that gets interviews

Hiring managers want **evidence you can ship reliable AI systems**, not certificates:
- **2–3 deep projects** beat 10 tutorials. Each should have: a real problem, a working demo, an **eval suite with numbers**, a cost/latency analysis, and a README explaining design trade-offs and failures.
- **Write about it**: a blog post or technical write-up per project ("How we cut order-parsing errors from 9% to 1.5%").
- **Open-source contributions**: fix an issue in an MCP server, an eval framework or an inference engine.
- Your capstone (this week) should be your flagship project.

## 13.4 Interview preparation

Typical loops include:
- **Coding**: standard algorithms/data structures plus practical tasks (implement attention, a tokenizer step, a retrieval function, an agent loop, a rate-limited API client).
- **ML/LLM fundamentals**: everything on this course's checklists — backprop, attention, scaling laws, RLHF/DPO, KV cache, quantization, RAG metrics.
- **System design for AI**: "Design an AI support agent for 10k restaurants", "Design RAG over 50M documents with tenant isolation", "Reduce LLM cost by 60% without hurting quality". Structure: requirements → architecture → data → model choice → evals → safety → serving/cost → monitoring → iteration plan.
- **Behavioral**: ownership, incidents you led, trade-offs you made. Prepare STAR stories from Wayfair and your own products.
- **Take-home or live build**: build a small LLM feature with evals in a few hours.

Practice explaining concepts at three levels: to a customer, to a PM, to a research engineer.

## 13.5 Keeping up without drowning

The field moves weekly. A sustainable system:
- **Primary sources**: lab research blogs and model/system cards, arXiv in cs.CL/cs.LG (skim abstracts weekly), conference proceedings (NeurIPS, ICML, ICLR, ACL).
- **Practitioner sources**: engineering blogs from AI labs and infrastructure companies, a few high-signal newsletters/podcasts.
- **Hands-on**: whenever a new model or technique appears, run it against *your* eval suite. Evals turn hype into information.
- **Fundamentals compound**: new techniques are usually recombinations of the ideas in Weeks 1–8.
